How to Spot a Fake Sale: 9 Signs a Deal Isn't Real

Big red tags and crossed-out prices are designed to make you buy fast. Here's how to tell a real discount from a marketing trick, and how to check any price before you pay.

Price Match Paradise Team Updated September 22, 2026 8 min read
Quick answer

A sale is fake when the discount is measured against a price the item was never really sold at, or when the "sale" price is no better than the regular price here or at other stores. The quickest test: compare the sale price with what the same item costs at other stores right now, and check the unit price.

9 signs a deal isn't what it looks like

1. The "was" price was never real

A big crossed-out price is only meaningful if the item actually sold at that price. The FTC's pricing guides say a former price should be the actual price at which the item was offered to the public on a regular basis for a reasonably substantial period of time. A price set briefly just to create a bigger "discount" doesn't count, but it still happens.

2. "Compare at" or "value" prices

"Compare at $89" or "a $120 value" doesn't mean anyone sells it for that. It's a reference price chosen by the seller. Ignore it and compare actual prices at other stores.

3. The sale never ends

If an item has been "on sale" every time you've seen it, the sale price is the regular price.

4. Countdown pressure

"Ends tonight!" and ticking timers are designed to stop you from comparing. A real deal will survive a 2-second price check.

5. Shrinkflation

The price is the same, or even "reduced," but the package is smaller. Always check the unit price (per ounce, count or sheet) on the shelf tag.

6. Multi-buy offers that aren't cheaper

"10 for $10" or "3 for $5" sounds like a deal, but check the single-item price. It's often the same per item, and at many stores you don't need to buy the full quantity to get the price. Read the tag.

7. Bundles you didn't want

Bundles hide the price of each piece. If you only need one item, a bundle can cost more than buying it alone.

8. Special "sale" versions

Some electronics and appliances are made in special versions for big sales, with different model numbers and sometimes lower specs. A familiar brand at a surprising price might not be the model you think. Compare by barcode.

9. Another store is simply cheaper

A deal can be a real discount at this store and still be more expensive than a regular price elsewhere. That's the most common "fake sale" of all, and the easiest to catch.

FAQ

Is it legal to show a fake "original" price?

In the US, the FTC's Guides Against Deceptive Pricing say a former price should be the actual price at which the item was openly offered on a regular basis for a reasonably substantial period. Fictitious former prices can be deceptive. State laws may add further rules.

How can I tell if a sale price is really a good deal?

Compare it with the current price of the same item at other stores, and check the unit price. If another store is cheaper at its regular price, the sale isn't the best deal.

Do I have to buy 10 items to get a "10 for $10" price?

Often not. Many stores apply the per-item price to any quantity, but some require the full amount. The shelf tag or the store's policy will say.

What is shrinkflation?

Shrinkflation is when a product gets smaller while the price stays the same or rises. Comparing unit prices is the easiest way to spot it.

Where can I report deceptive pricing?

In the US, at ReportFraud.ftc.gov or with your state attorney general's consumer protection office.
