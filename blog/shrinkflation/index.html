Shrinkflation: How to Spot It (and Stop Paying More for Less)

The box looks the same and the price hasn't changed, but there's less inside. Here's how shrinkflation works, how to catch it in seconds, and what to do about it.

Price Match Paradise Team Updated September 22, 2026 7 min read
Quick answer

Shrinkflation is when a product gets smaller (fewer ounces, sheets or pieces) while the price stays the same or rises. It's a hidden price increase. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics counts it as one in the Consumer Price Index. The easiest way to spot it is to compare the unit price (per ounce or count) instead of the package price.

What shrinkflation looks like

  • A 16 oz bag becomes 14.5 oz at the same price.
  • A 12-roll pack of paper towels keeps the same count but has fewer sheets per roll.
  • A cereal box stays the same height but gets slimmer.
  • A "family size" snack quietly holds fewer pieces.

Because the shelf price doesn't change, most people don't notice. The Bureau of Labor Statistics does: when a product shrinks and the price stays the same, the BLS records it as a price increase in the CPI, because you're paying the same for less. The U.S. Government Accountability Office also reviewed product downsizing trends in a 2025 report.

A related trick is sometimes called skimpflation: the size stays the same, but the product or service gets worse, for example cheaper ingredients.

How to spot it: use the unit price

The unit price is the price per ounce, per pound, per count or per sheet. It's usually shown in small print on the shelf tag. It's the only fair way to compare:

There's no federal requirement to show unit prices. According to NIST, nine states and Washington, D.C. require them, and other states set rules for stores that choose to show them. If the tag doesn't show one, divide the price by the size.

Where shrinkflation shows up most

It's most common in packaged goods where small size changes are hard to notice: snacks and chips, cereal, candy and chocolate, coffee, paper towels and toilet paper, detergent, ice cream, yogurt and personal care products.

What to do about it

  1. Compare unit prices, not package prices. Every time.
  2. Compare other sizes. After a downsize, a different size of the same product can be the better deal.
  3. Try the store brand. See Store Brand vs. Name Brand.
  4. Compare stores. A downsized product can still be cheaper per ounce at another store.

FAQ

What is shrinkflation?

Shrinkflation is when a product gets smaller while its price stays the same or goes up. You pay the same, or more, for less.

Is shrinkflation legal?

Generally, yes, as long as the package accurately states the net quantity. Some lawmakers have proposed disclosure rules, and a 2025 GAO report reviewed policy options.

How do I spot shrinkflation?

Compare the unit price (per ounce, count or sheet) instead of the package price, and check the net quantity on the package against what you remember.

Does inflation data include shrinkflation?

Yes. The Bureau of Labor Statistics monitors product sizes and records a smaller package at the same price as a price increase in the CPI.

Are stores required to show unit prices?

There's no federal requirement. According to NIST, nine states and Washington, D.C. require unit pricing, and other states set rules for stores that choose to show it.
