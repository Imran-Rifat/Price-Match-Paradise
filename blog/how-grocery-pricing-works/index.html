How Grocery Store Pricing Really Works (and Why the Same Item Costs Less Elsewhere)

The same box of cereal can cost noticeably different amounts at two stores a mile apart. It isn't random. Here's how stores set prices, and how to use that to pay less.

Price Match Paradise Team Updated September 22, 2026 10 min read
Quick answer

The same item costs different amounts at different stores because each retailer uses its own pricing strategy (everyday low prices or frequent sales), its own costs and competition in that area, and deliberate deep discounts on a few items to attract shoppers. Prices can even differ between locations of the same chain. The only reliable way to know which store is cheapest for a specific item is to compare the exact product.

Two pricing strategies: everyday low vs. high-low

Most grocery retailers lean toward one of two approaches:

Everyday low price (EDLP)High-low (Hi-Lo)
How it worksConsistently low shelf prices, fewer big salesHigher regular prices, frequent deep sales and coupons
Best forShoppers who want one predictable tripShoppers who plan around the weekly ad
Watch out for"Low" isn't always lowest on every itemPaying regular price on anything not on sale

That's why a Hi-Lo store can beat an EDLP store on the items in this week's ad and lose badly on everything else. Neither store is "the cheap one" for your whole list.

Loss leaders: the deals that get you in the door

Stores sometimes price a few popular items very low, occasionally at or below cost, to bring you in. Think front-page ad items, holiday turkeys, milk or eggs. The strategy works because most shoppers then buy the rest of their list at regular prices.

How to use it: buy the loss leaders, and compare the rest of the cart before you assume it's cheap too.

Why prices vary by location, even at the same chain

Many chains set prices by area, sometimes called zone pricing. Rent, wages, delivery costs and, most of all, local competition all affect what a store charges. A location with a discount competitor next door may be priced lower than the same chain's store across town.

That's also why national "which store is cheapest" rankings only go so far. What matters is the stores near you.

What shelf placement tells you

  • Eye level is prime space and often holds the brands and products the store most wants to sell. Manufacturers may pay for placement and promotions.
  • Top and bottom shelves often hold store brands and cheaper options.
  • Endcaps and displays look like deals, but they're often promotions rather than the lowest price in the aisle. Compare the unit price.

Store brands: where stores make their margin

Store brands (private label) usually cost shoppers less than name brands, and they're a growing share of what people buy. According to the Private Label Manufacturers Association, U.S. store brand sales hit a record $282.8 billion in 2025, reaching 21.3% of dollar sales and 23.5% of units. Package sizes, unit prices and shrinkflation

Different stores often carry different sizes of the same product, which makes shelf prices hard to compare. The unit price (per ounce, count or sheet) is the fair comparison. There's no federal requirement to show unit prices. According to NIST, nine states and Washington, D.C. mandate them, and others set rules for stores that choose to show them.

Package sizes also change over time. When a product shrinks and the price doesn't, that's shrinkflation, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics treats it as a price increase.

Digital shelf labels and changing prices

More stores are replacing paper price tags with electronic shelf labels that can be updated remotely. Retailers say this saves labor and keeps shelf and register prices in sync. Some shoppers worry it could make prices change more often. Either way, it's another reason to check the price when you're actually buying, not rely on what you remember from last week.

FAQ

Why does the same item cost different amounts at different stores?

Each retailer has its own pricing strategy, costs and local competition, and uses deep discounts on a few items to attract shoppers. Prices can even differ between locations of the same chain.

Do grocery prices differ between locations of the same chain?

Often, yes. Many chains price by area based on local costs and competition, so two stores of the same chain can charge different prices for the same item.

What is a loss leader?

A product priced very low, sometimes at or below cost, to bring shoppers into the store, on the expectation that they'll buy other items at regular prices.

Is an everyday-low-price store always cheaper?

Not on every item. A store with frequent sales can beat it on sale items, and EDLP stores aren't the cheapest on everything. Compare the specific items you buy.

Are stores required to show unit prices?

There's no federal requirement. According to NIST, nine states and Washington, D.C. require unit pricing, and other states set rules for stores that choose to show it.
